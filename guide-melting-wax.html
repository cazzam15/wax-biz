How to Melt Wax

Microwave, double boiler or electric melter? Which suits your wax and batch size, how to keep the temperature right, and how to stay safe at the melting stage.

Home / Buying Guides / Melting Wax

"What's a reliable wax melter for a beginner?" and "What do you melt your wax in?" come up in maker groups every week. The honest answer is that you don't need expensive kit to start. What matters is matching the method to your wax, watching the temperature, and never leaving hot wax alone. This guide walks through the three main options.

The three ways to melt wax

MicrowavePlant waxes (soy, rapeseed, coconut). Not paraffin.Testing scents and small first batchesUneven heating. Heat in short bursts and stir.
Double boiler (bain-marie)Plant and paraffin waxesRegular small batches at homeWater getting into the wax, and scalds from the hot water

Starting out: the microwave

UK supplier Candle Shack recommends starting with plant waxes, because they can be heated in a microwave, so you don't need special equipment. Put the wax in a microwave-safe container, heat it in short bursts, and take it out while a little solid wax is still there. Then stir until it has all melted before adding your fragrance oil. Candle Shack also notes that paraffin barely heats up in a microwave, so it can't be melted this way.

Keep a jug just for wax, not one you also use for food. Wax and fragrance oil are hard to clean off fully.

Getting regular: the double boiler

A double boiler (or bain-marie) melts wax gently with heat from hot water rather than a direct flame or hot plate, so it suits both plant and paraffin waxes. Candle Shack says a proper double boiler keeps the water sealed in a jacket, which lowers the risk of scalding and of water splashing into your wax, and holds around 1.2kg of molten wax.

If you use a pouring jug in a pan of water instead, keep the water low, never let the pan boil dry, and keep every drop of water out of the wax. Water in hot wax spits, and it can spoil the batch.

Growing: electric wax melters

Electric wax melting machines hold your wax at a steady temperature for hours. That lets you mix one big batch of wax and fragrance so every melt in it is the same. Candle Shack suggests one once you're selling or regularly waiting for wax to melt. Their iMelt 30, for example, holds up to 25kg. For most home makers that's a later upgrade, not a starting buy.

Whatever you choose, read the instruction leaflet for the temperature range and how full you can fill it, and only use it for wax.

Get the temperature right

Every wax is different, so there isn't one "correct" melting temperature. Your wax supplier's technical data sheet gives the temperatures to use for melting, adding fragrance and pouring. Use a thermometer and stay within them. Overheated wax can smoke, discolour and in the worst case catch fire.

Your fragrance oil's safety data sheet (SDS) lists its flash point in section 9, "physical and chemical properties". Add fragrance off the heat at the temperature your wax supplier recommends, and keep the SDS with your records. It's the same document your CLP label comes from (see our CLP and SDS guide).

Write the temperatures down for every batch. If a batch frosts or the scent is weak, your notes show what changed. Our free batch log does this, and the Batch Log & Recipe Book spreadsheet links every batch to its fragrance oil's SDS version. For faults, see the troubleshooting guide.

Melting safely at home

  • Never leave melting wax unattended, not even to answer the door. Turn the heat off if you have to walk away.
  • Never heat wax in a pan straight on the hob. Use a double boiler, a microwave (plant waxes only) or a melter made for wax.
  • Keep water away from hot wax. Lancashire Fire and Rescue Service warns never to add water to hot wax, as it can splash and burn.
  • Work on a clear, stable surface with nothing flammable nearby, and keep children and pets out of the room.
  • Keep a lid that fits your pot and a fire blanket close by.

🔥 If wax catches fire: treat it like a chip pan fire. The London Fire Brigade says: don't tackle it yourself and don't move the pan; never throw water on it, as it could create a fireball; turn off the heat only if it's safe to do so; then leave the room, close the door, warn others and call 999.

And for your customers

The melter your customers use matters too. Lancashire Fire and Rescue Service advises using a wax melter designed for wax melts, keeping it on a stable, heat-resistant surface, never leaving it unattended, and not melting wax for more than 4 hours at a time. Many makers add short "how to use" tips like these to their listings and leaflets, alongside the warnings your CLP label already carries.

Common questions

Can I melt wax for wax melts in the microwave?

Yes, if it is a plant wax such as soy, rapeseed or coconut. Candle Shack says plant waxes can be heated in a microwave in a microwave-safe container: take it out while a little solid wax is left, then stir until it has fully melted. Paraffin barely heats up in a microwave, so it can't be melted this way.

Do I need an electric wax melter to start a wax melt business?

No. Most makers start with a microwave (for plant waxes) or a double boiler. An electric wax melting machine is worth it once you make big batches often, because it holds wax at a steady temperature, so every melt in a batch gets the same mix.

What temperature should I melt wax melt wax to?

Use the temperatures on your wax supplier's technical data sheet. Waxes differ, so there is no single right number. Use a thermometer, never heat past what the supplier gives, and add fragrance at the temperature they recommend. Your fragrance oil's safety data sheet lists its flash point in section 9.

What should I do if melting wax catches fire?

Treat it like a chip pan fire. UK fire services say never throw water on it and don't move the pan. Turn off the heat only if it is safe to do so. If you can't put it out safely, leave the room, close the door, get everyone out and call 999.
