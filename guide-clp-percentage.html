10% or 25% CLP?

Which CLP percentage your wax melts need, how fragrance load changes the label, and what to do when you blend scents.

Home / Buying Guides / CLP Percentages

"Should I use the 10% or the 25% CLP?" is one of the questions UK wax melt makers ask most. The short answer: the percentage has to match, or be higher than, the amount of fragrance oil in your finished melt. This guide explains what the number means, why the warnings on your label change with your fragrance load, and what to do when you blend two scents.

What the percentage on a CLP means

A CLP label, or the CLP section of your supplier's paperwork, is worked out for a fragrance oil at a set concentration in the finished product. A 10% CLP is for a product that is 10% fragrance oil and 90% wax. A 25% CLP is for a product that is 25% fragrance oil. Many UK suppliers publish documents at 10% and 25%, and some at 100% (the neat oil).

Wax itself is usually non-hazardous. The warnings on a wax melt label come from the fragrance, and sometimes the colourant. HSE Northern Ireland puts it simply: "The greater the concentration of fragrance in the candle, the more likely it will trigger one or more of these warnings."

Why your label changes with your load

Most fragrance oils contain natural or synthetic ingredients that can cause skin allergies (skin sensitisers). Under CLP, what goes on your label depends on how much of each one ends up in the finished melt:

  • 0.1% or more of a sensitising ingredient (lower for strong sensitisers): the label needs the allergen statement "Contains [ingredient name]. May produce an allergic reaction."
  • 1% or more of a sensitising ingredient: the melt is classified as a skin sensitiser, so the label needs the exclamation-mark pictogram, the signal word Warning and H317: May cause an allergic skin reaction, plus the matching precautionary statements.

Other hazards, such as being harmful to aquatic life, have their own thresholds, which is why the pictograms can be different at 10% and at 25%.

💡 A worked example: imagine a fragrance oil that is 15% of one sensitising ingredient (made-up figures, for illustration only). At a 10% load, that ingredient makes up 1.5% of the melt, which is over 1%, so you need the pictogram, "Warning" and H317. At a 6% load it makes up 0.9%. That is under 1% but over 0.1%, so you may only need the "Contains … May produce an allergic reaction" line. The same oil can need a different label at a different load. Always use your supplier's figures, never your own estimate.

10% or 25%: which do you need?

UK suppliers such as Supplies for Candles and Scentify give the same rule: you can round your load up, but never down. A CLP worked out at a higher percentage can only over-state the hazards. A CLP worked out at a lower percentage than you use will leave warnings off your label.

Check your load with the fragrance load calculator, and make sure it doesn't go over your wax's maximum or the IFRA limit on your supplier's certificate. Our fragrance load guide explains why more oil doesn't always mean a stronger throw.

Rounding up is the cautious choice, but it can mean more warnings than your melt strictly needs. If you always pour at, say, 6% and want the simplest accurate label, ask your supplier whether they can provide CLP information at that load.

Blending scents: why you need a new CLP

Mixing two or more fragrance oils, for example a "sweet pea and vanilla" blend, creates a new mixture. HSE's guidance for GB downstream users (which includes small businesses that make products from chemicals they buy in) says you may use a classification that someone else in the supply chain has already worked out "provided you do not change the composition". Once you change the composition, the classification has to be done for the new mixture.

In practice, that means:

  • Don't use one oil's label for a blend. Each oil brings its own allergens, so a blend can need allergen names that neither single-oil label shows, and different pictograms.
  • Classify the blend at your total load. Two oils at 5% each is a 10% total load.
  • Get help if you need it. Some suppliers can calculate a CLP for blends of their own oils, and independent CLP labelling services and assessors can work from each oil's safety data sheet.
  • The same applies to anything else you add that has its own safety data sheet, such as some colourants or additives.

Safety data sheets & record keeping

Keep the safety data sheet (SDS) and CLP information for every oil, at the load you use, together with your batch records. The batch log makes it easy to see which oil and load went into each batch. HSE says downstream users must keep the information used for classification and labelling for at least 10 years after they last supplied the product, and should look out for updates. If a supplier sends a revised SDS, check whether your label needs to change.

You don't have to give an SDS to members of the public, as long as your label gives them enough information to use the product safely. If you sell wholesale to shops or other businesses, they can ask for an SDS for your melts and you must provide one. See our wholesale guide if you're starting to supply shops.

Selling to Northern Ireland or the EU

Sales in England, Scotland and Wales don't need a UFI code. Northern Ireland follows EU CLP, so hazardous wax melts sent there need a UFI on the label and a poison centre notification, as GOV.UK explains. Every different blend and load is a different formula, so it needs its own notification. Selling into the EU brings the same requirement, plus the EU General Product Safety Regulation. Our testing & safety guide covers both.

Common questions

Can I use a 10% CLP label for wax melts made at 6% fragrance?

UK fragrance suppliers say yes: a CLP worked out at 10% covers lower loads, because it can only over-state the hazards, never under-state them. A CLP worked out for your exact load is the most accurate, and may need fewer warnings.

Do I need a 25% CLP for wax melts?

Only if your fragrance load is above 10%. Most wax melts are made at 6–10%, so a 10% CLP usually fits. 25% CLPs are mainly meant for reed diffusers, room sprays and other products that use 15–25% fragrance.

Can I use one oil's CLP for a blend of two fragrance oils?

No. A blend is a new mixture, and HSE guidance says you can only reuse someone else's classification if you don't change the composition. Ask your supplier or a CLP labelling service to classify the blend at your total load.

Do I have to give customers a safety data sheet for my wax melts?

Not to members of the public, as long as your label gives them enough safety information. If a shop or other business buys your melts to resell, they can ask for a safety data sheet and you must provide one.
